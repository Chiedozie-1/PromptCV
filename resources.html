Career resources/Resume writingGUIDE · 5 MIN READ

Writing a resume with limited work experience

You can make a useful resume without a long list of formal jobs. Focus on relevant evidence of what you know, how you work, and what you have practised.

Start with what you have done

Consider coursework, personal or group projects, volunteering, caregiving, community work, freelance tasks, internships, and responsibilities outside paid employment. Include an experience only when you can describe your contribution honestly.

Choose a simple structure

  1. Contact details: your name, a professional email, and relevant location or portfolio link.
  2. Short profile: the role or direction you are pursuing and two relevant strengths.
  3. Education or relevant projects: place the section that best supports your application earlier.
  4. Experience and skills: show practical examples, not just a list of qualities.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE

“Organised a four-person class project, divided research tasks, and presented the final findings to the course group.”

This describes a specific action and context. Only use a statement like this if it accurately reflects what you did.

Before you send it: check the role’s requirements, confirm dates and details, and remove claims you cannot explain in a conversation.

Career resources/Resume writingGUIDE · 6 MIN READ

Tailoring your resume to a job description

Tailoring means making your most relevant, truthful experience easier to find. It does not mean changing your history to match an advert.

A practical four-step review

  1. Mark the essentials. Note the main responsibilities, required skills, and any stated qualifications.
  2. Find your evidence. For each important requirement, identify a real example from work, study, volunteering, or a project.
  3. Prioritise relevant details. Move the strongest matching experience higher and make each bullet clear about your action and contribution.
  4. Use familiar wording accurately. Include a role-specific term when it truthfully describes your experience; never add a skill you do not have.

A QUICK SELF-CHECK

  • Can I explain every skill listed?
  • Are the most relevant examples easy to find?
  • Have I kept dates, titles, and outcomes accurate?
  • Does the document stay readable as plain text?

There is no single resume format that guarantees a particular screening result. Clear, accurate information helps people understand your experience.

Career resources/Cover lettersGUIDE · 5 MIN READ

A clear structure for a focused cover letter

A cover letter adds context to an application. Keep it focused on why this role interests you and what relevant evidence you can bring.

Use three short parts

  1. Opening: name the position and briefly explain your interest.
  2. Evidence: choose one or two examples that connect your experience to the role.
  3. Close: thank the reader and state that you would welcome a conversation.

Example opening

“I’m applying for the customer support assistant role. I’m interested in work that combines clear communication with practical problem-solving, and I’ve developed those skills through my retail and community experience.”

KEEP IT PERSONAL AND TRUE

Use the organisation’s correct name and the role title from the advert. Do not claim knowledge, experience, or enthusiasm that you cannot support with a real reason or example.

Read the letter aloud once, check the recipient and role details, and make sure it adds something rather than repeating your resume line for line.

Career resources/Job searchCHECKLIST · 4 MIN READ

A manageable weekly job-search plan

A plan can help you keep track without treating job searching as a full-time job. Adjust the pace to your time, access, energy, and responsibilities.

Choose a few useful actions

  • Set aside time to look at roles from sources you trust.
  • Save roles worth reviewing and note their closing dates.
  • Check that each role matches your interests and current circumstances.
  • Prepare and review one application at a time.
  • Record where and when you applied, plus any next action.
  • Make time for follow-ups, interview preparation, and rest.

A SIMPLE TRACKING NOTE

For each application, note the organisation, role, link or contact, date, closing date, status, and next step. Keep personal information in a place you control and avoid sharing private application details unnecessarily.

Small, consistent steps can make your search easier to manage. There is no required number of applications to send in a week.

Career resources/Interview preparationGUIDE · 7 MIN READ

Preparing clear examples for an interview

Preparing a few real examples can make it easier to answer questions about how you approach work. Examples can come from employment, study, volunteering, or other relevant responsibilities.

Try the STAR structure

  • Situation: briefly describe the context.
  • Task: explain what needed to be done.
  • Action: focus on the steps you personally took.
  • Result: share what happened or what you learned.

Practise without memorising

Choose a few different examples, make short notes, and practise explaining them in your own words. Keep details accurate and respect any confidentiality obligations.

QUESTIONS TO CONSIDER

  • What interested you in this role?
  • Can you describe solving a difficult problem?
  • How do you organise competing priorities?
  • What would you like to ask the interviewer?

Interview processes vary. If you need an adjustment or clarification, contact the organisation using the information it provides.
